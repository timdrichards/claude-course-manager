# Situations

The recurring messages, and what each one has to accomplish.

---

## Deadline change

The most common announcement and the easiest to get subtly wrong.

Lead with the new deadline in the subject. In the body: the new date with day of week and
timezone, then explicitly what has not changed. When a deadline moves, the immediate question in
every reader's head is whether the next one moved too, whether the exam moved, whether late days
still apply.

Say why in one clause if the reason reflects well on the course ("since the autograder was down
Tuesday afternoon") and omit it if it does not need saying. Do not over-explain an extension;
students read a long justification as instability.

**Extensions granted because the course made a mistake should not consume late days.** Say so
explicitly. Students will otherwise assume the worst and some will not ask.

If anyone already submitted, say what happens to them: usually nothing, but silence on that point
generates messages.

---

## Correction to an assignment or spec

Structure: what was wrong, what it should say, what to do if you already did it the old way.

The third part is the one that gets left out and the only one that is actually urgent for the
students who are furthest along. A student who followed the old wording deserves to know whether
they need to redo work, and if the answer is "we will accept either interpretation", saying so
immediately prevents a weekend of unnecessary rewriting.

Do not be defensive about the error. One clause of acknowledgment, then the fix. "Requirement 4
said 'sorted' without specifying order; it should read 'ascending by name'. Either interpretation
will be accepted for this assignment."

If the correction changes the work substantially, pair it with a deadline change rather than
waiting to be asked.

---

## Exam logistics

The one message where completeness beats brevity, and the one worth checking twice, because every
missing detail becomes a hundred individual emails.

Cover: date, start and end time with timezone, room or rooms and how students know which,
what to bring, what is allowed (notes, calculator, laptop), what is covered, format and length,
accommodations arrangements and who to contact, and what to do if they are ill or unable to
attend.

Send it once well in advance and once again close to the date. The second one is not redundant;
it is when people actually read it.

Never invent a room, a time, or an accommodations process. Mark the gap and let staff fill it.

---

## Grade release

Say what was released, the distribution if the course shares one, how to read the feedback, and
the regrade process with its deadline.

Include the regrade window in the same message. Announcing grades without it guarantees a wave of
individual requests through whatever channel each student prefers, and a stated process with a
deadline converts that into something manageable.

If grading is not finished, say what is outstanding and when it is expected, rather than
releasing partial grades silently. Partially released grades look like a mistake to the students
who do not have theirs.

---

## A repeated question

When several students ask the same thing, the answer belongs to everyone. This usually points at
a lecture or spec gap rather than at the students.

Write it as a pinned post rather than an announcement when it is a clarification of something
already stated, and as an announcement when it is new information or changes what students should
do. The distinction matters: announcements notify, pinned posts inform whoever comes looking.

Keep the Socratic discipline in the answer only where the question is about graded work. A
question about a due date, submission format, or what a requirement means gets answered directly.

---

## Staff-facing messages

Different audience, different rules: TAs and graders need specifics, deadlines, and their own
name next to their piece of work.

Grading assignments to staff should say: which submissions, which rubric, by when, and where to
raise a question about an ambiguous case rather than deciding alone. That last one is what
prevents four graders quietly applying four standards.

Do not send staff messages through class announcements. Anything about grading standards, student
situations, or staff workload is not for students to read, and an announcement is permanent and
public.

---

## Mid-semester check-in

Optional and worth doing. Short: what is coming up, what to focus on, an honest word about where
the course is relative to plan, and an offer of help that names the actual channels.

Do not use it to scold the class about attendance, participation, or missed submissions. A
check-in framed as disappointment reads as hostility to the students who are doing fine and does
not reach the ones who are not.

---

## A course-wide grading error

Difficult, and the temptation is to minimize.

Lead with what happened and its scope: which assignment, which rubric item, how many students
affected, and in which direction. Then what is being done, and by when. Then what students need
to do, which should ideally be nothing.

**Scores that were too high are not lowered.** Fixing an error downward after grades are released
does more damage than the error, and most institutions have policy on this. State it plainly if
it applies, because students will assume the worst until told.

Do not send this until the fix is decided. An announcement that says "we are aware of an issue"
with no resolution generates a week of anxious messages.

---

## Academic honesty, class-wide

Rare and hazardous. Only send at the instructor's explicit direction, never on inference from a
grading batch.

Never characterize the scale, never suggest how many students are involved, never describe the
detection method, and never write anything that lets the class guess at individuals. The whole
class reads it, including the students concerned and their friends.

The useful version is forward-looking and short: a restatement of the policy, what the specific
boundary is on the current assignment, and an invitation to ask before rather than after. Anything
retrospective belongs in the institutional process, not in an announcement.

If the instructor's intent is to pressure suspected students into coming forward, say plainly
that this is the kind of decision that needs their judgment and probably their department's
guidance, and draft only what they direct.

---

## End of term

What remains, when final grades appear, how grades are computed, and what the process is for
questions after the course closes.

Include how to reach the instructor after the term ends, since course channels usually close and
students who need a reference or have a grade question find themselves with no way through.

Thanking the class is fine here and only here. It is one sentence.
